FROM CARD SCANNER TO CARD MARKETPLACE
The business plan — honest version, risks and kill-criteria included
July 7, 2026 · Internal, for the card show crew

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

THE THESIS, STATED CAREFULLY

"If people can make money on our app, they'll use it" is half right.
Transactions alone don't keep people around — liquidity (eBay) or
entertainment (Whatnot) does, and we have neither. What we DO have is
information nobody else has: what every user owns, what it's worth,
and — once shops are on board — who nearby will pay for it.

So the working thesis is:

    Money INFORMATION keeps people in the app.
    Money TRANSACTIONS are how we get paid.

Think Zillow: nobody sells their house this month, but everyone opens
the home-value email. We're that, for card collections — and the
marketplace harvests the intent the watching detects.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

1. WHAT WE ALREADY HAVE

- A scanner people actually use. Point the phone at a card, get its
  identity and market price in seconds. Also our secret onboarding
  weapon for shops (more below).
- Collections with live value. Users watch their collection's worth
  like a stock portfolio. That habit is the retention engine that
  everything else rides on.
- Wishlists. We know what people WANT, not just what they own.
- The show relationship. A real card show on AUGUST 27, organizers we
  know, vendors we can talk to. Supply, demand, and our users,
  physically in one room, on a date. Funded startups would kill for
  this.

2. THE MODEL — THREE LAYERS, ONE FLYWHEEL

LAYER 1 · WATCH (the daily habit)
Collection value tracking + wishlist & price alerts ("a card on your
watchlist dropped 15%", "your collection gained $120 this week").
Costs us almost nothing — we already sync prices daily. This layer is
what keeps the app on people's phones between purchases.

LAYER 2 · MONEY OUT (collectors get paid)
Partner shops post BUY LISTS (Japanese-shop style: "we're buying these
cards at these prices"). The app matches them against your collection:
"Shops at this show are buying 23 cards you own — about $612."
Later: CONSIGNMENT — sell your card through a shop's storefront; the
shop verifies and handles it, you get ~85% instead of a buylist's
~70%. We never touch the card or the money.

LAYER 3 · MONEY IN (collectors buy)
Browse partner shop and vendor inventory — before the show ("plan your
pickups"), at the show ("which booth has it"), and after (reserve from
a local shop, pick up in person). Payments stay between buyer and shop
until the loop is proven; in-app checkout with a platform fee is a
later phase, built only if reserves convert.

THE FREQUENCY PYRAMID IS THE POINT: people watch daily, buy monthly,
sell a few times a year. The watch layer carries the daily habit; the
marketplace layers monetize the intent it surfaces. We do NOT expect
buying/selling to carry retention — that bet killed most "sell your
cards" apps.

TRUST IS SHOP-ANCHORED, ALWAYS. Every card for sale and every buy
offer stands behind a real business with a reputation — no
stranger-to-stranger deals, no fakes under our banner. That's how we
borrow the trust of shows and shops without burning it.

THE TRADE MATRIX — one product, one rule: a vendor is always on one
side of every trade.

                     VENDOR BUYS              COLLECTOR BUYS
  COLLECTOR SELLS    buy lists + matching     never (P2P deferred —
                     (the lead wedge)         the trust cliff)
  VENDOR SELLS       free byproduct (dealer-  listings + browse +
                     to-dealer just happens)  reserve

Collectors make money AND spend money — always with a real business
across the table. Vendors source inventory AND move inventory, which
is literally what a dealer is (buy at 70%, sell at market; we put
rails under their spread). Vendor-to-vendor rides along free — a
vendor browsing another vendor's listings is just a user. The
collector-to-collector cell stays deliberately empty until we've
earned verification muscle.

THE FLOW — ONE COLLECTOR'S SHOW WEEK:

  TUESDAY (home)    Scans their binder on the couch — no kiosk. A
                    vendor scans new inventory + posts a buylist rule.
  THURSDAY          Notification: "Vendors at Saturday's show are
                    buying 14 cards you own — about $612. Two wishlist
                    cards are for sale at the show." Now they know
                    what to bring and what to budget.
  SATURDAY (show)   The app is the map of the room: which booth is
                    buying their cards, which booth has the card they
                    want. Walk over, cash out, buy the card. No money
                    touches us.
  MONDAY (after)    Nothing dies: collection, wishlist, and shop pages
                    persist — see a card Tuesday, reserve, pick up
                    Saturday. The watch layer keeps the daily pulse.

WHAT KEEPS PEOPLE OPENING IT BETWEEN SHOWS (STICKINESS, HONESTLY)

The marketplace itself will never be sticky — transactions are
episodic. Shows are acquisition SPIKES, not the retention engine.
The retention stack, in order of how often it fires:

1. THE EVERYDAY QUESTION (constant): "what's this card worth?" is the
   most-asked question in the hobby — pack openings, trade nights,
   LGS visits. Our scanner is the fastest answer that exists, and
   scanning a card is how it enters your collection.
2. THE WATCH LAYER (daily/weekly): collection value movement,
   wishlist price alerts, owned-card spikes. Zillow psychology —
   nobody transacts monthly, everybody checks. Prices already sync
   daily; this is a query plus a notification.
3. SET RELEASES (quarterly rhythm): every new set is a usage spike —
   people scan their pulls. The app should be where pulls go.
4. MATCHING JOLTS (episodic, high-voltage): "a shop near you raised
   its buy price on 3 cards you own." Rare, screenshot-worthy.
5. THE LOCAL LAYER (monthly): shop pages persisting between shows.

The design consequence: THE SHOW EXPERIENCE MUST END WITH THE
ATTENDEE'S SCANNED CARDS SAVED AS THEIR COLLECTION. An attendee who
installs, browses, and never scans their own cards retains at layer
zero — nothing ever fires for them again. Layers 1-4 all feed off
collection data. Week-4 retention after the show (Test #1 below) is
the number that says whether we're sticky or just show-popular.

3. THE CARD SHOW PLAYBOOK — AUG 27 IS OPENING NIGHT

NOW → EARLY AUGUST
Talk to vendors and shops: who's in, buylist interest, freshness rules
agreed as partnership terms. Schedule scanning sessions — a shop's
inventory gets listed BY SCANNING IT, binder to storefront in an

EARLY → MID AUGUST
App goes PUBLIC (App Store, production backend, frictionless
first-run) — a show full of people who can't install the app is
pointless. Founding partners populate inventory + buy lists while the
marketplace is still hidden from buyers.

SHOW WEEK
The hook we advertise: "See every card for sale at the show — and find
out which vendors will buy yours — before you walk in." At the show,
the app is the map of the room: which booth has your card, which booth
is buying your cards.

AFTER THE SHOW
Shop pages persist and it becomes the LOCAL marketplace: browse
Tuesday night, reserve, pick up Saturday. Reserve/contact flows ship
here — at the show itself, people just walk to the booth.

SHOW TWO AND BEYOND
The playbook repeats — ideally sold THROUGH THE ORGANIZER: every booth
registration includes a "digital booth" in the official show app,
organizer takes a cut. The organizer becomes our sales force.

4. WHO SELLS — SHOPS, VENDORS, AND THE HYBRIDS WE WANT MOST

CARD SHOPS      Permanent store, fixed address, local reputation.
                Trust: highest. Offer: full storefront — inventory +
                buy list + consignment anchor; persists after every
                show.

SHOW VENDORS    Cases and a table; gone on Sunday. Trust: lowest.
                Offer: lightweight "digital booth" for the show
                weekend; buy list posting (low commitment).

HYBRIDS         Shop owners who also work show booths. Best of both —
                these are the ideal founding partners.

HOW MANY AT LAUNCH: 3–5, HAND-PICKED. Each founding partner needs
hours of personal onboarding and a scanning session. Five shops at
1,000–3,000 cards each is 5–15k listings — plenty for a show. Ten
half-onboarded partners is worse than four done right.

First job for everyone reading this: help figure out which August 27
vendors run permanent shops.

5. CONSIGNMENT — HOW CASUAL COLLECTORS SELL WITHOUT US TOUCHING MONEY

The collector picks a card from their collection in the app → chooses
a partner shop → sees the shop's terms up front (say a 15% cut) →
hands the card over at the store or the show → THE SHOP INSPECTS AND
VERIFIES IT → it's listed under the shop's storefront tagged
"consigned" → it sells like any shop card → the shop pays the
collector. The hobby already works this way (consignment cases, COMC);
we're putting rails under existing behavior.

The collector's ladder:
  SELL NOW   → to a buy list, ~70% of market, cash today
  CONSIGN    → ~85% of market, wait for the sale
  P2P        → maybe someday, only when we've earned verification
               muscle

Known frictions we handle in shop terms, not code: liability while the
shop holds the card, automatic price-decay so cards actually move, a
90-day unsold policy. Consignment is v2 — founding shops get
comfortable with plain listings first.

6. HOW WE MAKE MONEY — THE REVENUE MENU, WITH HONEST VERDICTS

The strongest fact in this plan: WE'RE REDIRECTING MONEY THAT ALREADY
MOVES. Vendors pay $200–500 per booth. Shops pay for ads and foot
traffic. Nobody has to invent a budget — just route existing spend
through something that measurably works better.

DIGITAL BOOTH FEE                                    [ SHOW 2+ ]
~$50 per vendor per show to be browsable in the app; ideally bundled
into booth registration via the organizer. Congruent with money
vendors already spend — and MARKET-VALIDATED: Heystack charges
vendors $49.99 for show access (see COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE below).
First show is free for founding partners — we're collecting the proof
we'll charge against.

SHOP STOREFRONT SUBSCRIPTION                         [ FALL 2026 ]
Flat monthly (start $50–100, grow to ~$200) for unlimited listings +
buy list + the match traffic we send. Only sellable with receipts:
"we sent 60 people to your store last month." Founding partners are
free during the pilot — honest metrics beat $200 checks.

TRANSACTION FEES                                     [ PHASE 2 ]
In-app checkout (Stripe), ~5–8% platform fee taken automatically. The
real marketplace business — but it brings disputes, refunds,
chargebacks, and sales-tax obligations. Built only after
reserve-and-pickup demonstrably converts.

CONSIGNMENT CUT                                      [ PHASE 2+ ]
A small slice of the shop's consignment fee. Rides on transaction
rails; not chased before them.

PER-CARD LISTING FEES                                [ NEVER ]
The most-hated pricing model in the hobby, and it taxes the exact
thing our cold start needs: supply.

REALISTIC MATH, NO BULLSHIT: one metro's show scene at decent
penetration — ~30 digital booths a show plus ~10 shop subscriptions —
is roughly $2–3K/MONTH PER METRO. That's a genuinely good bootstrap
business if the playbook repeats across shows and cities; it's a big
business only when transaction fees arrive and the app is the
collection layer shops depend on (exactly the position TCGplayer paid
to acquire into — see BinderPOS). Near-term we're optimizing for proof
and users, with revenue starting at show two.

And the burn is nearly zero: no payments means no fraud or dispute
staff, no subsidies, infra is a couple hundred a month. The downside
case is a summer of nights, not anyone's savings.

7. COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE — HEYSTACK "SHOW CONNECT" (July 2026)

Heystack (an existing dealer-tools company, "Heystack One" scanning
stations) launched Show Connect at the West Coast Card Show: attendees
scan cards at PHYSICAL KIOSKS on the show floor; vendors pay $49.99
(capped at 50 vendors) to browse those scans, set filter alerts, make
offers, and ask sellers to bring cards to their booth. Sold through
the show organizer's own vendor mailing list.

WHAT IT VALIDATES FOR US (a competitor just market-tested our plan):
- Vendors pay ~$50 for show-scoped access to attendee inventory —
  our digital-booth price point, confirmed with real money.
- Sell-side first is the right wedge — their entire product is the
  sell-side; no buyer marketplace at all.
- The organizer channel works — they used the organizer as their
  sales force, exactly the play we designed.
- Even the revenue math: 50 vendors × $50 = $2,500/show, matching our
  per-metro estimate.

WHERE WE'RE STRUCTURALLY DIFFERENT (mostly stronger):
- They need kiosks; we live in the attendee's pocket. Their matching
  can't start until someone walks to a station at the show. Ours
  starts on the couch on Tuesday — "know what to bring before doors
  open" is a funnel they can't run.
- Their experience dies Sunday night. Stations leave; attendees walk
  away with nothing installed. We keep the collection, wishlist,
  watch layer, and shop pages on Monday — retention is our moat and
  nobody else is building it.
- They're one-directional (vendor buys from attendee only). We're
  two-directional with the same trust rule.

WHAT IT CHANGES:
- URGENT: lock our show organizer NOW ("official show app",
  exclusivity if possible) — before Heystack or someone like them
  emails OUR show's vendor list. This moved from Phase-0 item to
  this-week item.
- Pricing confidence: non-founding vendors at show two can be priced
  at $50, not $25 — the anchor is set.
- Feature to adopt later (Phase 2): vendor OFFERS on scanned cards.
  Buylist rules for scale, offers for heat — "a vendor made you an
  offer" is a hotter notification than a passive buylist match.
- Watch them: if Heystack ships a consumer app that keeps attendee
  collections alive after the show, they converge on us from the
  vendor side. Their DNA is dealer hardware; consumer retention is a
  different muscle. Our race is to own the attendee's pocket first.

8. RISKS WE'RE NOT HIDING FROM

COLD START. With 3–5 shops, browse selection is thin — a buyer who
searches and finds nothing doesn't come back. Answer: lead with
MATCHING, not browse. Rules-based buy lists ("we pay 70% of market on
these sets") cover thousands of cards, so "shops are buying N cards
you own" delivers big, real numbers from day one, while sell-side
inventory grows.

STALE LISTINGS. A reserved card that already sold in-store kills trust
fast. Answer: confirmation windows, listings that expire unless the
shop one-tap renews them, and per-shop reliability tracking — agreed
up front as partnership terms, not discovered as penalties.

THE GRAVEYARD. Dozens of "sell your cards" apps died; even TCGplayer
shut its own buylist down. They died betting transactions would retain
users. Our retention bet is the watch layer, which works with zero
marketplace activity.

INCUMBENTS. TCGplayer or Collectr could go local, and Heystack is
already at shows (see COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE). Our defense isn't
features — it's shop relationships, show-floor presence, and owning
the attendee's pocket, which are slow, physical, and unglamorous to
copy.

PRICE-DATA QUALITY. Our market prices drive real money decisions now.
Known-bad segments (some Japanese raw-card prices) get fenced off from
any percent-of-market math before matching goes live. Hard gate, no
exceptions.

ONE-PERSON EXECUTION. Seven weeks to public launch, vendor onboarding,
and the show. Survivable only with ruthless scope: buy lists and
browse before reserve; consignment in v2; payments later.

9. FIVE TESTS THAT DECIDE WHETHER THIS IS A BUSINESS

1. Do attendees KEEP the app after the show?
   Pass: healthy week-4 retention, carried by collection value +
   wishlist alerts.

2. Do matches convert to VISITS?
   Pass: founding shops literally count walk-ins who say "the app
   sent me."

3. Do vendors/shops PAY at show two?
   Pass: digital booths and subscriptions bought with their own money.

4. Does the playbook REPEAT without personal favors?
   Pass: a show where we don't know the organizer runs the same play.

5. Do reserves CONVERT to completed sales?
   Pass: the green light (or not) for building in-app payments.

If matches fire and nobody visits, or nobody pays at show two — we
learned it for the cost of a summer, and the scanner/collection app is
still a great product. That's the whole risk posture: every step is
cheap, dated, and measurable before the next one gets built.
